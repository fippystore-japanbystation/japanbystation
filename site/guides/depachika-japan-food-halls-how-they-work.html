Depachika 101: How Japan's Department-Store Food Halls Work

Down the escalator, floor by floor: what's there, when the stickers go on, where to eat it, and the tax rule that just changed.

Floor by floorTastings & markdownsTax-free

If you only do one food thing in a Japanese city that isn't a restaurant, make it the basement of a big department store. A depachika is dozens of separate counters under one roof: a famous yokan maker next to a bento kitchen next to a wine cellar. Walk it in the order below, go in the last hour if you want deals, and keep your receipts if anything is going home in your suitcase.

Floor by floor: what's actually down there

We'll use Isetan Shinjuku's B1 as the model, because its floor guide spells it out. Most big stores follow the same pattern.

  • Sweets first. Western pâtisseries on one side, wagashi and tea on the other. This is where the gift boxes live. At Nihonbashi Mitsukoshi, Toraya's bamboo-wrapped yokan "Yoru no Ume" is ¥3,024 and a Mon Cher Dojima Roll is ¥1,782.
  • Prepared food and bento. Isetan has about 50 deli and bento shops. Daimaru Tokyo, right on top of Tokyo Station, says its B1F brings in about 1,000 new bento a year, some made in front of you. It's the place to buy lunch for the Shinkansen.
  • Bakeries. Isetan alone has seven.
  • Fresh market. Fruit, fish and meat sold like a very good grocer. Mitsukoshi's buyers list Asakusa Imahan wagyu for sukiyaki at ¥10,800 for 600 g.
  • Sake, wine and craft beer. Isetan's wine and spirits corner holds about 1,900 bottles and has a sommelier.

One counter that surprises first-timers is Mitsukoshi's Kayuan: it sells regional sweets from all over Japan, so you can taste a Kyushu or Tohoku speciality without leaving Tokyo.

Free tastings and the evening markdowns

Tastings. The Japan National Tourism Organization tells visitors to keep an eye out for free samples. Time Out Tokyo found tasting counters in Isetan's fresh market, from ham and sausage to oden. Not every tasting is free: some counters run paid tastings of their most expensive items. Take a sample, say thank you, and don't feel you have to buy.

Markdowns. No store publishes a time, and each counter decides its own cut. Time Out suggests going 30 to 60 minutes before closing, when most ready-to-eat food is discounted. A 2013 President report on Tobu Ikebukuro found ¥100-off and half-price stickers half an hour before its 21:00 close. Bento, sushi and deli go first. Sweets and gift boxes keep longer, so they rarely drop.

Our suggestion: sweets and gifts in the morning, when the shelves are full. Come back for dinner food in the last hour.

Eat it there, or take it away?

Mostly take-away, and eating by the display cases is frowned upon. In 2021, Tokyu Toyoko got so many complaints about shoppers eating by the counters that it added standing tables with bins. Look for the proper eat-in spots instead:

  • Isetan Shinjuku B1: a Kitchen Stage whose chef's menu changes every two to three weeks, a craft beer bar, wine tasting counters, Jean-Paul Hévin's chocolate bar and Toraya's café.
  • Daimaru Tokyo: some B1F bento shops have small counters for food made to order.
  • Mitsukoshi: HiO Ice Cream has an eat-in (¥495).

Paper bags on the Mitsukoshi Isetan food floors have cost a little extra since 2020 (¥30–50 when the fee started), so bring a foldable bag.

📅 Good to know: tax-free changed on 1 November 2026

  • You now pay tax at the till. Under the Japan Tourism Agency's refund system, the store registers your purchase. You get the tax back after customs confirms the goods are leaving Japan: scan your passport at the airport, before you check your bags, within 90 days of buying.
  • Minimum ¥5,000 before tax, same store, same day. Food and other items now count together, and the old sealed bags are gone (National Tax Agency).
  • Eat it here and the refund is gone. Food eaten in Japan doesn't qualify. If any item from a transaction is missing at customs, nothing on that transaction is refunded, so keep gift boxes together and unopened.
  • Stores can take a cut. Takashimaya, for example, registers purchases at its counter on the day of purchase and deducts a handling fee of 23% of the tax amount.

One more thing: the sweets that arrive on set days

Some cult sweets don't come in every day. Mitsukoshi's buyers note that Kamakura Benitaya's Kurumikko (walnut caramel cakes) is restocked on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays. Akasaka Kakiyama's Himeenza rice crackers aren't sold at all from November to January. If a box is on your list, check the store's restock days before you go. Kyoto's station store runs a whole schedule like this (see our Kyoto Station food hall guide).
