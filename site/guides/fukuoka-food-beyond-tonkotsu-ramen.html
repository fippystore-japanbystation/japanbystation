Fukuoka Food Beyond Tonkotsu Ramen: Eat It Like a Local

Udon soft on purpose, mackerel raw, noodles at the end of the hotpot. Six dishes, and the way Fukuoka actually eats them.

UdonHotpotsRamen

If you only try one unusual thing in Fukuoka, make it goma saba: raw mackerel in sesame-soy, something most of Japan doesn't eat raw because mackerel spoils so fast. Then eat the familiar things the local way: udon soft, hotpot finished with noodles, ramen ordered by firmness. Here are six dishes, with a well-known place and a smaller one for each.

1. Udon, soft on purpose

Visitors expect firm, chewy udon. Hakata udon is soft and tender, in a clear broth of flying fish, dried anchovy, bonito and kelp, and the standard topping is goboten, burdock tempura (Go Fukuoka). Hakata even claims the dish's origin: Jotenji temple has a stone marking it as the birthplace of udon and soba in Japan. The local order adds kashiwa meshi, rice cooked with chicken.

  • Well-known: Makino Udon, Hakata Bus Terminal B1F, 2 minutes from Hakata Station. 10:00–23:00, about ¥1,000 or less, cash only. Each bowl arrives with a small kettle of extra broth, because the noodles keep drinking it up (Japan Guide Map).
  • Local: Daichi no Udon, Fukuoka Asahi Building B2F, 3 minutes from the Hakata Exit. 10:00–16:00 and 17:00–21:00, every day, meal-ticket machine, cash only, 33 seats (Tabelog).

How to eat it: don't rush. Letting the noodles soften is the point; top up the broth from the kettle as you go.

2. Goma saba: mackerel, raw

Raw mackerel sashimi dressed in sesame and soy, eaten with rice or with sake. Mackerel goes off fast, so eating it raw is a luxury that Fukuoka's fresh catch from Hakata Bay and the Genkai Sea makes possible (Ministry of Agriculture). Locals make it at home, too.

  • Well-known: Kiharu no Gomasaba-ya, Haruyoshi, 5 minutes from Tenjin-Minami Station. Dinner only, Monday to Saturday 17:30–22:30, about ¥4,000–5,000 with a ¥500 table charge (Tabelog).
  • Local: Hakata Gomasaba-ya, Maizuru, 5 minutes from Akasaka Station. Lunch 11:00–14:00 and dinner 17:30–21:30, Monday to Saturday (the shop says Sundays and holidays are now lunch only). Goma saba ¥1,000 (Tabelog).

How to eat it: go at lunch for the cheapest way in, with rice, and mix the sesame sauce through before you start.

3. Motsunabe: the noodles come last

A soy-based hotpot of fresh beef offal and plenty of vegetables. The local move is the finish: once the offal is gone, champon noodles go into the broth. Ganso Motsunabe Rakutenchi, founded in Tenjin in 1977, says this habit started with its owner, who tried champon from the Chinese restaurant downstairs (Japan Guide Map); its courses include all-you-can-eat champon. Rakutenchi has several branches, so check the one you pick.

  • Local: Hakata Motsunabe Yamanaka, Ohashi main shop, 10 minutes from Nishitetsu Ohashi Station. From 16:00 on weekdays, lunch too on weekends and holidays, closed Tuesdays. Courses ¥4,980 and ¥5,980 (Tabelog).

How to eat it: pace yourself. The champon is the best part.

4. Mizutaki: drink the soup first

A chicken hotpot with a cloudy, rich broth. Hakata style starts with a cup of the plain soup before anything goes in, then the chicken, then the vegetables, so the broth gets sweeter as you go (Hanamidori on Gurunavi). It ends with zosui, rice cooked in what's left.

  • Well-known: Hakata Hanamidori, Hakata Station Chikushi Exit shop, 5 minutes. Hana course listed at ¥5,500, lunch 11:30–15:00 and dinner from 17:00 (an older listing; check before you go).
  • Local: Mizutaki Nagano, Tsushimakoji, 10 minutes from Nakasu-Kawabata Exit 7, about 100 years old. Monday to Saturday 12:00–22:00, closed Sundays. Bone-in course ¥3,300; dip the chicken in vinegar-soy with grated momiji radish (Tabelog).

5. Mentaiko: breakfast, not a souvenir

Spicy cod roe. Fukuya's founder, Toshio Kawahara, based it on a Korean dish he ate as a child, first sold it at his Hakata grocery in 1949 and spent over ten years perfecting the flavour (Fukuya). Most visitors buy a box at the airport. Locals eat it with rice.

  • Well-known: Fukuya's Nakasu main shop, weekdays 9:00–22:00, Sundays and holidays 9:30–18:00.
  • Sit-down: Ganso Hakata Mentaiju, Nishinakasu, 5 minutes from Tenjin or Nakasu-Kawabata. Open 7:00–22:30 every day, about ¥2,000–3,000. A whole kombu-wrapped roe sits on rice and you choose one of four sauce strengths (Japan Guide Map).

How to eat it: as breakfast. Mentaiju opens at 7:00, and at lunch you can take a numbered ticket instead of standing in line.

6. The classic, ordered properly: tonkotsu ramen

Yes, eat the ramen. But order it like a local. Noodle firmness runs from nama (barely cooked) through harigane, barikata, kata, futsu (normal), yawa to bariyawa, and locals tend to go a little firm. When the noodles are gone, keep the soup and order kaedama, a second serving of noodles. It began with Nagahama ramen, whose very thin noodles go soft quickly (Crossroad Fukuoka).

  • Late night: Hakata Ramen Shin-Shin, Tenjin main shop, 4 minutes from Tenjin Station Exit 4, 11:00–3:00, closed on irregular days.

How to eat it: order the kaedama one step softer than your first bowl. The soup has cooled by then, so the noodles soften less.

One more thing: ramen at 6 a.m., like the fish market

Ganso Nagahamaya started in 1952 as a street stall feeding workers from the Nagahama fish market, and it still opens at 6:00 (to 1:45; closed 31 December to 5 January). Buy a ticket from the machine, tell the staff your firmness, and you'll be eating with a few early workers instead of a queue. It's in Trust Park Nagahama 3, 10 minutes from Akasaka Station, and a visit costs about ¥1,000.
